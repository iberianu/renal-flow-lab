The Kidney as a Diagnostic Tool

What the kidney does to a solute is written in its clearance — the volume of plasma scrubbed clean of it each minute. Compare a solute's clearance to GFR and RPF and you can read how the tubule handled it: that's how inulin measures GFR, PAH measures RPF, and sugar in the urine flags diabetes.

What leaves in the urine: E = F − R + S
FFiltered load = plasma conc. × GFR — what the glomerulus lets through
RReabsorbed — returned from the tubule to the blood
SSecreted — added from the blood into the tubule
EExcreted = F − R + S — what ends up in the urine
CClearance = E / P — the volume of blood plasma from which the substance is completely removed by the kidneys per unit of time (e.g., mL/min)

Kidney clearance as a tool

Follow a marker through one nephron. It arrives in the blood (P × RPF), some is filtered at the glomerulus (P × GFR), and — if the tubule secretes it — more is pulled from the peritubular blood into the tubule. What stays in the blood leaves by the renal vein; what reaches the end of the tubule is excreted. Switch between creatinine/inulin and PAH to see why one measures GFR and the other measures RPF.

Creatinine as a clinical GFR gauge

Creatinine is produced steadily, so at steady state plasma creatinine is inversely proportional to GFR: P_Cr = k / GFR. The curve is steep at low GFR — early disease barely moves creatinine, while a small later drop sends it soaring.

Renal blood flow as a circuit

The renal circulation obeys Ohm's law. The pressure drop from renal artery to renal vein is the voltage source, blood flow is the current, and each vascular segment is a resistor wired in series. Change the source pressure or the two arteriolar resistances and watch the current (RBF) — and the flow speed in the loop — respond.

Ohm's law for the kidney (V = IR)
RBF = ΔP ⁄ Rtotal = (PA − PV) ⁄ (Ra+Rgc+Re+Rv)
IRBF — renal blood flow (the current)
VPA − PV — artery minus vein pressure (the source)
RRa, Re — afferent & efferent arterioles (the control points); Rgc, Rv (glomerular capillary + venous) stay fixed and small

Mechanisms

Two effectors: the myogenic response (a stretched afferent arteriole contracts) and tubuloglomerular feedback (the macula densa senses distal flow and signals the afferent arteriole). Both act on the afferent side.

Mass balance

Whatever enters the kidney in the arterial blood must leave — either in the renal vein or in the urine. That single accounting rule is mass balance, and writing it out for any substance X is what lets clearance measure kidney function. Toggle between PAH and inulin/creatinine to see how the same equation yields two different flows.

Whole-kidney mass balance
PX,a·RPFa = PX,v·RPFv + UX·V̇U
arteryPX,a · RPFa — X delivered by the arterial plasma
veinPX,v · RPFv — X leaving in the renal vein
urineUX · V̇U — X excreted in the urine
Substance X = the marker being tracked (e.g. PAH, inulin) · in (arterial) = out (renal vein + urine)

About the model. The numbers here are produced by simplified mathematical models written into the code — they aim to resemble real clinical values but are not 100% clinically accurate. Glomerular pressure and flow use a series-resistance divider: renal blood flow = (P_art − P_venous) ⁄ (R_a + R_e), P_GC is set by the afferent/efferent balance, and RPF = (1 − Hct) · RBF. GFR is computed from the Starling equation, GFR = Kf·[(P_GC − P_BS) − (π_GC − π_BS)], where net filtration pressure (NFP) is the driving force; filtration runs only when NFP > 0 and GFR is capped at RPF. Solute handling uses mass balance, E = F − R + S with clearance C = E/P, and the two-marker panel derives clearance from P_a·RPF = P_v·RPF + U·V̇. Extreme slider positions are there to expose trends and won't always land on physiological values.
